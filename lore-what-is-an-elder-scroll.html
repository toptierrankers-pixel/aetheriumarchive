What Is an Elder Scroll, Actually? The Lore Explained

The artifact the entire series is named for, and one of the least understood ideas in its lore.

Ancient, unwritten, and older than history itself

Elder Scrolls are physical artifacts said to predate the Aurbis, the entire structured universe the setting takes place in. They're described as fragments of unwritten possibility rather than a conventional record of events, which is part of why reading one doesn't work the way reading a normal book does. They don't record what happened. In some sense, they contain what could happen, which is exactly why interpreting one is treated as a genuinely dangerous, specialized skill rather than something any literate person can just sit down and do.

Who can actually read them

Most attempts to read an Elder Scroll end in blindness, at least temporarily. The Cult of the Ancestor Moth, commonly called Moth Priests, are the closest thing the setting has to trained specialists, and even they typically go blind from prolonged exposure, relying on trained moths to help interpret the Scrolls' contents indirectly rather than reading them directly with unprotected eyes.

What they're actually used for in the games

Elder Scrolls show up as major plot devices more than once. In Skyrim, reading one at a specific ancient location allows the Dragonborn to glimpse a vision tied to the Dragon War, a pivotal moment used to advance the main quest. Across the series more broadly, they're associated with triggering or explaining Dragon Break events, the time-fracturing moments we cover in detail in our Dragon Break guide, where more than one version of history briefly becomes simultaneously true.

Why the series is named after them

Beyond their in-universe function, Elder Scrolls work as a fitting emblem for the whole franchise's relationship with its own lore. The setting is famously unreliable on purpose, told through biased in-world texts, contradictory historical accounts, and events that different characters remember differently. An artifact that shows fragments of possibility rather than settled fact is a genuinely clever thing to name an entire series after.
