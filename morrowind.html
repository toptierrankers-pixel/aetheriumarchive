The Elder Scrolls III: Morrowind (2002)

Smaller than Daggerfall, and stranger than anything the series has made since.

A deliberate step away from scale

After Daggerfall's continent-spanning map, Bethesda made a pointed decision with Morrowind: go smaller and go stranger. Instead of a procedurally generated world, the island of Vvardenfell was hand-built from the ground up, letting the team fill it with giant mushroom towers, ash storms, floating cities, and a culture that owed nothing to standard medieval-fantasy tropes.

Setting: Vvardenfell

Morrowind drops the player into Dunmer (Dark Elf) territory as a released prisoner sent on a quiet mission that quickly turns into a prophecy-driven confrontation with the god-king Vivec and the ancient evil Dagoth Ur. The politics of House Redoran, House Hlaalu, and House Telvanni give the story real texture, and the game trusts the player to figure most of it out through reading and exploration rather than constant hand-holding.

Why fans still call it the best

Ask a longtime Elder Scrolls fan which game they'd defend as the series' peak, and Morrowind comes up constantly. Its lack of fast travel (aside from an in-world network of silt striders, boats, and mage guild teleporters) forces genuine exploration. Its writing leans literary rather than cinematic. And its setting still hasn't been matched by anything in the series since, including by Morrowind's own return as a province in The Elder Scrolls Online.

Should you play it today?

Yes, though go in prepared for an older style of RPG. Combat resolves on hidden dice rolls rather than pure aim, quests rarely mark exact locations on your map, and reading NPC dialogue carefully actually matters. For players willing to meet it halfway, Morrowind remains one of the most distinct worlds in the genre.
